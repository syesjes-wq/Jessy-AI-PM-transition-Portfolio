# Origin and Research

## How it started

Before there was an app, there was a whiteboard on the kitchen wall.

I put it up to solve a simple problem: every morning with my two boys — aged 4 and 6 — was a battle. Getting dressed, packing bags, brushing teeth. Tasks that should take ten minutes were taking thirty, with reminders and resistance the whole way through.

The whiteboard tracked habits. Stars were given for completing them. Rewards were promised for reaching certain totals.

Two weeks in, I had enough observations to know this was worth building properly.

---

## What I observed

These are real moments from the first two weeks of running the manual star chart at home. Each one directly shaped a product decision.

---

### The 6am morning

On day 14, I came back from the gym to find both boys already dressed and ready for school — at 6am, without being asked.

The night before, my elder son had calculated how many stars he needed to reach his reward. He told his younger brother they should wake up early to make sure they earned them.

I had not designed this. The system created the motivation and they figured out the rest.

**What this taught me:** When the motivation belongs to the child, they will exceed what you ask of them without being asked. This became the benchmark for every product decision — *would this feature create that 6am morning?*

---

### The elder coaching the younger

The same night before the 6am morning, my 6-year-old told his 4-year-old brother to wake up early so neither of them would lose their star balance.

No leaderboard. No sibling feature. Just a shared family view where each child's progress was visible to the other — and peer accountability happened naturally.

**What this taught me:** Sibling transparency creates coaching without any designed mechanic. The product does not need to engineer this — it just needs to make each child's progress visible to the other.

---

### The negative star for missing the bus

One rule in our house: miss the school bus, both boys lose 1 star.

It created more behaviour change than weeks of verbal reminders. The consequence was small, consistent, and tied to something the boys genuinely cared about.

**What this taught me:** Negative stars work — but only when used sparingly, consistently, and for something the child actually values. Used too often, they lose meaning. The product should allow them but with a guardrail: a parent note explaining why, visible to the child.

---

### "I earned this — I'll keep it in a money purse"

My 4.5-year-old received cash as a reward. He immediately asked for a money purse to store it in.

Nobody taught him what earning meant. The system made it feel real enough that he invented his own relationship with it.

**What this taught me:** The product does not need to teach concepts like earning or saving. It just needs to make the earning feel tangible. The child does the rest.

---

### The monster truck savings goal

My 6-year-old decided not to redeem his stars for a smaller reward. Instead, he set a target of 60 stars for a monster truck — a multi-week goal he invented himself.

A 6-year-old, practising delayed gratification, because the system made the larger goal feel achievable.

**What this taught me:** Build an official savings goal feature. Let the child set a target, see a visual progress bar, and feel the anticipation build week over week. The function is simple. The feeling it creates is the point.

---

### The shared experience problem

One child earned enough stars to claim a trip to the play area. The other had not earned his stars that day. He did not mind the toy his brother got — but he could not stop crying about missing the play area. You cannot split an experience.

**What this taught me:** Family experiences need a different redemption mechanic — one where each child has their own star target, both must reach it, and the parent fulfils it once for the whole family. This is a V2 feature, directly validated by a real moment.

---

### The joint savings goal

Unprompted, both boys proposed pooling their stars — 1,000 each — to buy one expensive toy together: a remote-control transformer car at 2,000 stars combined.

**What this taught me:** Children will naturally want to collaborate toward shared goals. A joint goal feature — where multiple children contribute to a shared pot and both get the celebration — is not a product idea. It is something my kids asked for without knowing it was a product feature.

---

## What two weeks of real use produced

- Two boys waking at 6am independently
- A 6-year-old coaching his 4-year-old brother
- A 4.5-year-old understanding the concept of earning
- A 6-year-old practising delayed gratification
- Two stress-free school mornings

A whiteboard, a marker, and consistent follow-through. HabitlyKids is that whiteboard, built properly.
